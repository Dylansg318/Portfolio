## The story

On a marketplace like Net32, the seller with the right price wins the sale, and
competitors change their prices all day long. Ours were set from a spreadsheet someone
updated every morning by eyeballing the competition. By lunchtime it was stale. By the
next morning we'd either lost sales overnight or quietly given margin away.

So I built the robot version of that morning routine. Every minute it looks at the
products that are due for a check, sees what competitors are charging right now, and
decides: move, or hold. It has manners built in. It never drops below the floor price a
human set, it waits twelve hours before touching the same product twice, and it never
plays "match the lowest," because two robots doing that race each other to zero profit
in an afternoon.

It has made over **138,000 price changes** so far, and every single one is written down
with the reason, so when someone asks "why is this listed at $41.20?", the answer takes
one look, not an argument.

</PlainOnly>

## Where it started

The first thing I built at RMH3 was a Python and Flask tool that scraped competitor prices
with XPath and pushed updates through the marketplace API. It replaced the spreadsheet and
it worked, but it lived beside the rest of the business rather than inside it, and as the
ERP grew it became the second place prices were decided.

## The schedule

Every product/vendor pair gets its own row in a schedule table: an anchor time, a 12-hour
cooldown, and the last price we pushed. A sweep runs every minute and only touches the rows
that are due. For each one it scrapes the live market, runs the strategy, and pushes to the
marketplace only if the answer changed *and* the cooldown has cleared. Every attempt, push
or hold, lands in a log with the reason.

That log is the most-read table in the system. When someone asks "why is this listing at
$41.20?", the answer is one query away, with the competitor prices the engine saw when it
decided.

## The strategy

"Match the lowest" is what everyone asks for and what nobody actually wants. Two sellers
running it against each other converge on zero margin in an afternoon. The tiered strategy
instead:

1. Identifies the buy-box winner in the mode we care about (authorised sellers by default).
2. Undercuts them by a configured step, but only down to the product's floor.
3. Holds if the floor would be breached, or if the winner is us, or if nothing has moved
   since the last push.

Floors and ceilings are per product and set by a person. The engine never invents a floor.

## One engine

The rule that prevents the most bugs is boring: there's exactly one function that computes
a price, and every path calls it. The manual "Execute" button, the bulk push, the ⚡ Auto
toggle, the recompute that runs after a scrape, and the "next run preview" card on the
listing page all go through the same code. If the preview says $41.20, the job will push
$41.20.

<Takeaway>
  Before this rule the dashboard showed an "optimal price" computed one way and the job
  pushed a price computed another way. They agreed most of the time, which is the worst
  possible amount, often enough to be trusted, rarely enough to be caught.
</Takeaway>

## Health, not just price

The same engine feeds a buy-box analysis every five minutes, so the intelligence feed can
say which listings lost the box, which competitors are new, and where our floor is what's
holding us back. Pricing overrides, contract pricing, temporary promos, stay manual on
purpose. Those are judgment calls. The engine's job is to make them visible, not to make
them.

</EngOnly>

Nothing moves a price without writing down why it moved. That is what turns a wrong price
into a five-minute question instead of an afternoon, and it is why the log is a screen you
can open rather than a table someone digs through after something has already gone wrong.
