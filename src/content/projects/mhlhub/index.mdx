## The story

RMH3 Dental sells dental supplies to dentists across the country — on Net32, eBay,
Amazon, Shopify, Walmart, and over the phone. When I joined, each of those lived in its
own tool, plus a dozen spreadsheets and a shared email inbox someone sorted by hand.
The tools disagreed with each other every single day, and someone had to figure out
which one was lying before the trucks left.

I was hired to fix one spreadsheet — the one where prices got updated every morning.
Instead, piece by piece, I built the company a single system that does all of it: every
order from every website lands in one place; the warehouse picks and packs from one
screen; prices update themselves; shipping labels get bought and printed automatically;
returns and customer emails live in the same place as the orders they're about; and the
accounting matches the sales, with the receipts to prove it.

The result you can measure: the company went from about 270 orders a day to about 520 —
almost double — **without hiring anyone new in operations**. And when a number looks
wrong now, there's one system to ask, and it shows its work.

The part I'm proudest of isn't a feature. It's that the expensive mistakes — the ones
where a number is *believably* wrong and nobody notices for a month — now have guardrails
built in, because each one only ever got to happen once.

## What the business needed

A dental supply distributor lives on thin margins and high volume. When I started, about
270 orders a day came in across five marketplaces and a phone line, and each one passed
through three or four tools on its way to a box. Inventory was in one place, prices in
another, the books in a third, and when they disagreed — which was daily — someone
reconciled by hand at the end of the shift.

The brief was small: fix the pricing spreadsheet. The real job turned out to be everything
around it.

## How it's shaped

MHLHUB is a single TypeScript codebase — an Express API, a React client, one PostgreSQL
database — deployed as a container on Railway through GitHub Actions. That part is
ordinary on purpose. The interesting decisions are in how it's organised.

**Seven loops, not a feature list.** Every screen and every one of the 180 scheduled jobs
belongs to one of seven daily loops: order intake → fulfilment, inventory and procurement,
repricing, customer service, returns, financials, and intelligence. If a proposed feature
doesn't slot into a loop, that's a signal to stop and ask what it's for. It keeps the
system from growing sideways.

**Seams, not pages.** Before building something new, the question is whether an existing
seam covers it — the job scheduler, the signals feed, the document-ingestion queue, the
audit log. "A page that lists things the user then does something about" almost always
wants to be a signal producer, not a page.

**Jobs that survive a redeploy.** Every long-running job checkpoints. When Railway swaps
the container mid-run, the next run resumes from the last checkpoint instead of starting
over — or worse, silently skipping the tail.

## The rules that stopped the bleeding

The rules that matter most are the ones that cost money before they existed. A few:

- **The product master is sacred.** Marketplace syncs write to a listings table and never
  to the catalog. Before this rule, a bad feed could rename 20,000 products overnight.
- **Business date, not record date.** Revenue, velocity and ship-time metrics window on when
  the order happened, never on when it landed in our database. Backfills insert months after
  the sale; a plain filter once overstated a month of revenue by 60%.
- **A cart on Net32 is two orders.** Net32 splits one checkout across our two storefronts
  and gives both the same order number. Finance, inventory and customer service treat them
  as separate; only the shipper may box them together. Every query on that order number
  must also say which storefront, or refuse.
- **Humans see the channel's order number, never ours.** An alert that says "order 1323434"
  is unactionable — nobody can type that into eBay. Every human-facing surface names the
  marketplace's id and the storefront.

<Takeaway>
  None of these are clever. Each exists because its absence produced a specific, expensive
  mistake, and each is enforced in code — a query helper, a hook, a schema constraint — not
  in a wiki nobody reads.
</Takeaway>

## What it runs today

Order intake from six channels with per-channel identity rules. Inventory with lots and
expiry, reservations, cycle counts, and a push of stock levels back to every marketplace.
A repricing engine that runs every minute. Rate shopping and label purchase across USPS,
UPS and FedEx, with thermal printing on the warehouse floor and proof of delivery pulled
from carrier polls. A customer-service desk over Gmail and eBay messages, with returns and
refund splits. Invoicing, AR and AP, and a two-way QuickBooks Desktop integration. A
signals feed that surfaces what a human should look at today.

About 520 orders a day go through it now — roughly double where it started — with the same
operations team.
