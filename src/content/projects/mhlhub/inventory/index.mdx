## The story

Some products matter more than others. A few hundred of them earn nearly all the money,
and the rest sit on a shelf being occasionally useful. Knowing which is which is what
tells the warehouse what to count this week and what to reorder before it runs out.

A job runs every night to sort that out, marking each product A, B or C. It had a bug that
is almost funny in hindsight: it was picking the top 20% of *everything we could sell*,
about 27,000 products — but only about 3,700 of those had sold anything at all. Twenty
percent of 27,000 is bigger than 3,700, so every product that had ever sold got marked
"most important". Nearly two thousand top-priority products, and three that weren't.

Nothing broke. No error, no alert. It just quietly made two other things wrong. The
reordering maths gave every product the caution normally reserved for the critical ones,
and rounded every suggested order up — so we bought more than we needed, across the board.
And the instruction "go count the important items" pointed the warehouse at five thousand
products, more than three thousand of which had never sold a single unit. People were
counting shelves that didn't need counting, and the shelves that did were in the same pile.

The fix is one sentence: rank only the products that actually sell. That turns the list
into what it was always supposed to be — **745 products earning 89% of the money**, then a
middle group, then a long tail.

There's a related habit at the other end of the building. When the scanner reads a barcode
it isn't sure about, it says so instead of guessing. It used to occasionally read part of
our own product code as a batch number and show a batch number that didn't exist. A
missing batch number makes somebody walk over and look at the box. An invented one doesn't.

## A percentile over the wrong population

The classifier's job is ordinary Pareto: sort by revenue velocity, take the top slice as A,
the next as B, the rest C. The bug was in what got sorted. It ranked every sellable product
— 27,215 of them — so a 20% A bucket meant 5,443 slots. Only 3,721 products carried any
demand in the lookback window at all.

When the bucket is larger than the population that can fill it, the boundary never binds.
Everything with a pulse landed in A:

- **1,979** class A
- **2** class B
- **3** class C

That distribution is the entire finding. Every assertion you would naturally write — that
the sort is descending, that the buckets partition the input, that percentages sum to 100 —
passes on it.

## What broke downstream, quietly

Nothing consumed the classes directly. Two things consumed them indirectly, and both failed
in the direction that costs money without announcing itself.

**Replenishment.** The suggested-quantity SQL picks a service level (a Z score) from the
class. With everything in A, the service level pinned at 1.96 for the entire catalog and
the suggested quantity always rounded up. Over-ordering across the board, permanently, and
each individual suggestion looked perfectly reasonable in isolation.

**Cycle counting.** The scheduler's rule is "count the A items often". That instruction now
pointed at 5,443 products, more than 3,700 of which had never sold a unit. Counting effort
went to the shelves least likely to be wrong and least costly if they were, while the
products that actually move sat in the same undifferentiated pile.

Ranking only products with demand produces the split the system was designed around:

| Class | Products | Share of 30-day revenue |
|---|---|---|
| A | 745 | 89.0% |
| B | 1,117 | 9.5% |
| C | 1,859 | 1.6% |

## Why zero demand lands in C, not null

The obvious modelling choice for "this product has no demand, so it wasn't ranked" is null.
It is also the wrong one, in two specific ways.

The cycle-count schedules filter on class. A null empties the C bucket entirely, so the
long tail stops being scheduled for counting at all — it doesn't get counted rarely, it
gets counted never. And the replenishment SQL has a fallback branch for a missing class
that routes to the B service level of 1.65, which is a *larger* safety cushion than C. Null
would have quietly ordered more stock of the products with no demand whatsoever.

So no-demand products floor to C. It looks like a shortcut in the code, which is exactly
why the reasoning sits in a comment directly above it.

<Takeaway>
  A distribution can pass every unit test and still be impossible. Anything that ranks or
  scores gets its output buckets counted, not just its logic asserted.
</Takeaway>

One smaller edge in the same function: classes resolve by product id, not by index into the
caller's ordering. The function sorts internally so it doesn't depend on the caller's
`ORDER BY`, and a credit line with negative revenue — sorting below all the zeros — can't
shift the class boundaries underneath everything else.

## Reading what's physically on the shelf

Inventory is only as good as what gets recorded when a box is opened, and the failure mode
there is the same shape: a confident wrong answer beats a missing one only in the mind of
whoever wrote the parser.

The scanner reads GS1 Application Identifiers off cartons — GTIN, batch/lot, expiry. Twice,
internal SKUs beginning "106-" were read as AI (10), the batch/lot field, and put a
fabricated lot number onto screens people act on. The fix is an anchoring rule: an AI
sequence is only trusted where the payload's structure actually places one, never inferred
from a number that happens to start with the right digits. The HIBC `$$` date layout is
deliberately not inferred either, because a wrong expiry on a dental consumable is worse
than no expiry.

Every degradation lands in the same place — a literal-only lookup, which produces a miss.
A miss sends a person to go and look at the box. A fabricated lot number sends nobody
anywhere.

The classifier and the scan parser are both public in
[`code/`](https://github.com/Dylansg318/Portfolio/tree/main/code), with the measured
numbers and the traps in the comments.
