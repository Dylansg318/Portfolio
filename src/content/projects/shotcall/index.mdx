A ball rolls onto a billiards table at forty-five degrees. It bounces three times
off anything solid — the rails, or the block bolted to the cloth — and drops into
the first pocket it reaches. There are fourteen pockets. You get four guesses, and
each wrong one rolls the ball forward by one bounce, so a miss buys you information
instead of just costing you a life.

The name is the pool rule it borrows: under call-shot rules you have to nominate
the pocket before you shoot, and the cushions on the way, which is exactly what
this asks of you.

You can also chalk the route yourself. Drag across the cloth and the line follows
your hand — no snapping to the grid, no smoothing — and when the real path appears
you get both lines together and a number for how far off you were. That turns out
to be a much harder question than which pocket it lands in. One board a day, four
tries, and then it is spent until tomorrow — at which point the table builds its
own. The pockets shut, the block drops through the cloth, a panel slides over the
hole, another opens somewhere else, and the new pockets are fired out of the
middle and bounce off the rails until they settle into the rim.

That is the entire game, and it is not the interesting part. The interesting part
is that a daily puzzle needs a board every day, forever, and the constraint that
makes it scoreable is the same constraint that makes boards scarce.

## Where a new puzzle comes from every day

The hard part of a daily puzzle isn't the puzzle. It's having a fresh one every
morning, forever, that's *the same kind of hard* as yesterday's — otherwise the
score you post doesn't mean anything.

Everything here has exactly three bounces. That's what makes Tuesday comparable to
Monday, and it's also what nearly killed the idea: on a plain rectangular table
there are only about 91 genuinely different puzzles. Three months and the game is
finished. Putting one solid block in the middle of the table took that to 1,037 —
a bit under three years — and it asks nothing new of the player, because bouncing
off solid things is the entire game already.

The puzzle-maker is brute force and makes no apology for it. It builds every
combination of table size, block shape, block position, and where the ball comes
in — **170,356 possible puzzles** — rolls each one, and throws away anything not
worth playing:

- **100,472** because the ball hits an exact corner, where it bounces off two walls
  at once and you can't tell by looking what happened.
- **39,964** because one of the four legs is too short to notice or too long to
  count comfortably.
- **12,092** because the ball never touches the block, which makes it decoration
  that looks like it matters.
- **5,352** because the ball ends up on the block instead of in a pocket.
- **4,140** because the answer sits too close to where the ball came in, so you'd
  get it by feel.
- **1,488** because the ball would roll straight over its own answer earlier in the
  journey — meaning the stated answer is a lie.
- **456** because fourteen properly spaced pockets won't fit.

That leaves 6,392 — and then the surprise. **84% of those were mirror images of
each other.** A puzzle flipped left-to-right is the same puzzle with the letters
moved, so only one of each pair survives. Final count: **1,037 genuinely different
puzzles, about 0.6% of what it started with.** The "five years of puzzles" I
thought I had was under three.

The last step sorts all 1,037 by how hard they are, cuts them into seven equal
groups, and gives each day of the week its own group — easiest on Monday, hardest
on Saturday, Sunday a step back down, the way crosswords do it. So "Saturday"
means the genuinely hardest seventh of what exists, not a difficulty number I
invented. All 365 days got one from the group they asked for.

There is a second thing to do with a board besides guessing. Drag across the
cloth and you draw on it, freehand, exactly where your hand went — so you can
trace the route you think the ball takes. When the real path appears you see both
lines at once and get a number for how far off you were, in dots. Getting the
pocket right is luck one time in fourteen; getting the route right is not. And if
you want another go at the drawing, an eraser drops onto the line and scrubs at one
small spot — and the whole line comes off anyway, in three goes, the way a hand
clears a board.

When the day's board is spent there is a button that builds another one on the
spot. It is not a softer version to keep you busy: a board made in the browser has
to pass the same seven tests as one from the year, so it is a real puzzle — it just
does not count towards the day. Watching it happen is half the fun, because the
table clears itself like a machine: the pockets close, the block sinks through the
cloth, a green panel slides over the hole it left, another section opens up
somewhere else, the block rises back through it, and then the new pockets are shot
out from the middle and bounce off the walls until each one lands where it belongs.

And because a puzzle that sends the ball to the wrong pocket is worse than no
puzzle, none of this is taken on trust. Every one of the 365 days that shipped is
re-played by a separate checker that confirms the ball still lands where the
puzzle says it does — and the build refuses to finish if a single one doesn't.

## The rule everything rests on

The ball travels the forty-five degree lattice: one unit sideways for every unit
forward, always. That single restriction is what makes the whole thing exact. The
ball lands on every whole number of dots along both axes as it goes, so no
position is ever a fraction, it can never slip through a surface before touching
it, and there is no tolerance anywhere in the code to tune.

A block face needs no rule of its own either. An axis-aligned surface flips the
perpendicular component of the motion and leaves the parallel one alone, whichever
side of it you are standing on — so the block's vertical faces do exactly what the
left and right rails do, its horizontal faces do what the top and bottom do, and
its corner flips both. That is why an obstacle in the middle of the table adds
nothing for the player to learn.

## Why the tables are odd sizes

The first thing I assumed was that a bigger table means a harder puzzle. It
doesn't. The governing variable is the greatest common divisor of the two sides,
and it decides how much of the rim the ball can ever reach:

| gcd(W, H) | share of the rim the ball can reach |
|---|---|
| 1 | ~50% |
| 2 | ~41% |
| 3 | ~34–38% |
| 4 | ~28% |
| 6 | ~19% |

A shared factor closes the ball's cycle early, and once the cycle closes the ball
retraces a path it has already walked. So a 12×6 table has more cloth than an
11×9 and is worth less: fewer reachable pockets, fewer distinct boards, and a
visible bias in where the ball tends to end up. Every table in the shipped set —
10×7, 11×7, 11×8, 11×9, 10×9, 12×7, 13×9, 13×10 — is coprime, and the generator
throws if anyone adds one that isn't.

## Where the boards come from

The generator is brute force with hard filters, and it is not apologetic about it.
It builds every combination of table size, block shape, block position, entry
point on the rim and inward diagonal — 170,356 boards — walks each one, and throws
away almost all of them:

| what happened | boards |
|---|---|
| considered | 170,356 |
| a bounce landed exactly on a corner | −100,472 |
| a leg shorter than 2 dots or longer than 7 | −39,964 |
| the ball never touches the block | −12,092 |
| the fourth contact was the block, not a pocket | −5,352 |
| the answer sits too close to the entry | −4,140 |
| the ball crosses its own answer early | −1,488 |
| fourteen spread pockets would not fit | −456 |
| **survived** | **6,392** |
| mirror images of a board already kept | −5,355 |
| **shipped** | **1,037** |

Corners alone kill 59%. A ball arriving exactly at a corner reflects off both
faces at once, which is correct physics and unreadable on screen — you cannot tell
by looking whether it bounced or reversed — so every one of them goes.

Two of those filters exist because a puzzle has to be *honest* rather than merely
solvable. A board where the ball rolls over its own answer on an earlier leg would
have dropped in there, so the stated answer is a lie; 1,488 of those. And a board
whose block the ball never touches is worse than a board with no block at all,
because it is furniture that looks like it matters; 12,092.

Then the surprise. 84% of everything that passed every filter was a mirror image
of a board already in the list. A table flipped left-to-right is the same puzzle
with different letters on the pockets, so each board is reduced to an identity
under all four reflections of a rectangle and only one member of each family
ships. That is what turned "about five years of supply" into 2.8 years, and it is
the number I would have got wrong if I hadn't checked.

## The elegant version, and why it isn't the one that shipped

On a bare rectangle you never simulate anything. Unfold the table — mirror it
repeatedly instead of bouncing the ball — and the path becomes a straight line, so
rail contacts fall exactly where `t` is congruent to minus the entry coordinate,
modulo the width or the height. The whole contact sequence is two arithmetic
progressions merged together, and you can read off the answer in closed form with
no loop at all.

The block destroys that. Its faces are not on either progression, and a bounce off
one restarts the ball from a new point in a new direction, so every progression
downstream of it would have to be re-derived. The version that shipped steps the
ball one diagonal unit at a time and checks what it is touching.

That is less clever, and it was the right call. The entire search space is about
eight million steps, which runs in a fifth of a second, so the closed form would
have bought nothing measurable and introduced a second way to be wrong. Keeping it
would have been keeping it for my benefit, not the game's.

## Grading a year of days

A difficulty score out of thin air would be a number I made up. This one is
weighted by what each factor actually costs the player: the total number of dots
to count, the single longest leg — one long count is where you slip — how many
bounces are off the block, since you have to notice it before you can predict it,
how uneven the legs are, and how crowded the answer's neighbourhood is, because a
lone pocket is easier to land on.

The scores then get split into seven **equal-count** tiers rather than fixed
thresholds, which matters more than it sounds. It means Saturday is the hardest
seventh of the boards that actually exist, not the boards that clear a cutoff I
guessed at. Monday through Saturday ramp up and Sunday steps back down — the
crossword habit — and a deterministic shuffle inside each tier means the same
commit always deals the same year. All 365 days drew from the tier their weekday
asked for; none had to fall back to a neighbour.

The answer is deliberately not in the file. The browser has to trace the path
anyway in order to animate it, so storing the pocket index would be redundant data
that also happened to be tomorrow's answer. The generator asserts it instead.

Where I'll be straight about a limit: a prerendered site with no server ships the
whole year's *boards* to anyone who opens the demo, so a determined reader can
work out next Tuesday for themselves. That is inherent to the architecture, and a
puzzle for fun doesn't need me to pretend otherwise.

## Drawing your own line

Naming a pocket is a one-in-fourteen guess. Drawing the route is not, so the cloth
takes a freehand line: the points are wherever the pointer actually went, with no
snapping to the lattice and no smoothing, because the whole value of the thing is
a hand-drawn curve sitting next to an exact one.

Scoring it is where the judgement is. The obvious measure — average distance from
each drawn point to the nearest point on the true path — is worthless, and
worthless in a way that looks fine until you try to cheat it. A one-inch scribble
sitting on top of the path scores nearly perfect, because every point of the
scribble is indeed close to the path. So the measure runs both ways: the drawing
against the path *and* the path against the drawing, averaged. The second half is
what makes a line that didn't go anywhere score like one, and it costs nothing —
the path is sampled every quarter dot, which is about a hundred points against a
few hundred drawn ones.

Drawing is a toggle rather than always-on, and that is a phone decision. A finger
can only draw where the page has given up its own scrolling (`touch-action: none`),
and the cloth is most of a phone screen — always-on would mean a reader who
scrolled onto the table could not scroll off it. So it defaults on for a mouse,
where a drag never scrolled anything, and off for touch, and either way it is one
tap. A tap on a pocket still guesses while chalk is armed, because a tap is not a
stroke: strokes under two dots of travel are discarded, which is also what stops a
stray twitch from wiping a line someone worked on.

Two bugs in that only existed on the input I wasn't using, and both were invisible
to reading the code:

- **Capturing the pointer on `pointerdown`** retargets the `click` that follows it
  to the capture element, so every pocket silently stopped accepting a tap the
  moment drawing was armed. Capture now happens on the first *move*, by which point
  it is a stroke and the click doesn't matter.
- **Calling `preventDefault()` on `pointerdown`** suppresses the synthesised click
  altogether, so on a phone a finger tap on a pocket did nothing at all. That call
  moved to the move handler too.

The erase is one eraser that does not travel. It lands on the middle of the line
and shakes — the tight, slightly impatient motion of someone rubbing at one small
mark — and the entire line degrades anyway, wherever on the board it happens to be.
The gap between a tiny gesture and a total effect is the joke, and it only works
because the eraser stays put.

It is still three discrete scrubs with a beat of nothing between them, because a
fade is what a computer does and a board being cleared goes in strokes. The line is
redrawn as short pieces rather than one path so it can *break* rather than dim: each
scrub takes whole pieces out, leaves the survivors on a broken dasharray with a
thinner stroke, and puffs dust at the eraser. Faded-but-solid chalk does not read as
chalk.

The first version of this swept across the table instead, and it carried a real bug
that is worth recording because the fix was to delete the mechanic. A piece in the
last few percent of a sweep is only damaged if an animation frame happens to land on
it, and pinning pieces to "damaged by this pass only" then excluded anything the
sweep flew past from every later pass — so the erase finished with a bright,
untouched tail still standing. I patched that by letting a later pass collect an
earlier one's misses. Then the eraser stopped travelling, damage stopped depending
on position at all, and the entire class of bug went with it.

## The table that racks itself

Past the daily board there is a button that generates another. Two things had to
be true for that to be worth shipping.

**It has to be the same game.** A practice board built in the browser must clear
the same bar as one dealt from the year, or it is a softer game wearing the same
clothes. So the definition of a fair board — the sizes, the eight filters, the
pocket layout, the difficulty score — moved out of the build script into a module
the demo imports. The enumerator and the browser now call the same `evaluate`, and
regenerating the year after the move produced a byte-identical file, which is how
I know the extraction changed nothing.

**Generation is rejection sampling, not enumeration.** The browser does not need
the space, it needs one board now: pick a random block shape, position, entry and
direction, judge it, and try again if it fails. About 3.7% survive, so it lands in
roughly 27 attempts — measured at **0.039 ms per board**, which is below the
threshold of anything.

The board's difficulty is scored by the same function as the dealt ones, but a
browser-made board has no idea where it falls in a pool it never saw. So the six
scores that separate the seven tiers now ship in the JSON, and a practice board
names its own tier on exactly the same scale.

It re-racks the *same table*, deliberately. Changing the size mid-animation would
not be a re-rack, it would be different furniture — and keeping W and H fixed also
means the frame, the projection and the pocket letters are all stable, so only the
block, the entry and the pockets have to move.

### The animation is the game's own mathematics

The last phase fires fourteen pockets out of the centre and bounces them off the
rails until each lands on its exact spot in the rim. Simulated bounces would land
wherever they landed. These land where the pocket belongs, because the path is
*folded* rather than simulated: take the straight line from the centre to a
**mirrored image** of the target, then fold the result back into the table with a
triangle wave, and you get a genuine bouncing path that terminates exactly on the
target. That is the unfolding trick that makes a 45-degree billiard path solvable
in closed form, and the same idea as the diamond system a player uses on a real
table — used here as an animation.

Two bugs in the transition, both worth naming because both were invisible in the
code and obvious on screen:

- **A gradient measured against the wrong box.** The re-rack slides cloth panels
  over the cloth, and an SVG gradient defaults to `objectBoundingBox` units — so a
  small panel got the entire sweep of light squeezed into it and read as a
  differently-lit patch sitting on the table. Anchoring the gradient to the
  table's own coordinates (`userSpaceOnUse`) makes a panel pixel-identical to what
  it covers, and only its leading edge gives the movement away, which is exactly
  the mechanical look.
- **Clearing opacity is not clearing content.** The chalk path, the bounce marks
  and the crossed-out pockets were faded to nothing in the first phase and then
  restored at the end — still holding the *previous* board's drawing, which
  painted the old puzzle onto the new table. The reset had to happen at the moment
  the board swaps, not when the animation stops.

And the gate grew a third section. It now generates **2,000 boards through the
runtime path** — 250 per table size — and re-derives every rule from scratch
rather than trusting the function that claims to enforce them. A practice board
that quietly skipped a filter is exactly the kind of thing nothing else would
catch.

## The test that was wrong

The physics gate checks seven invariants over every board shape the generator can
produce — 25,448 boards and 1.55 million ball positions, in 0.19 seconds. Six of
them are the obvious ones: the ball stays on the table, never ends up inside the
block, never tunnels through a face, every step is exactly one diagonal unit, the
direction flips at a surface and only at a surface, and it never turns in open
space.

The seventh is the one that earns its keep. Run the path backwards from its end
point and it must retrace itself exactly. A reflection that is wrong at any single
face or corner breaks that, and nothing else will catch it.

It failed on 32% of boards, and the test was the thing that was broken. Leaving a
contact point in reverse means negating the direction the ball *arrived* on, not
the one it bounced away on — at a contact those are two different rays. My first
version negated the wrong one. Changing a test until it passes is usually how you
ship a bug with a green checkmark, so it mattered to be able to say exactly which
line was wrong and why the physics was never in question.

## One copy of the rule

Three things have to agree about where the ball goes: the generator that deals a
year of boards, the gate that proves the physics, and the demo the browser runs.
For a while each of them had its own copy of the reflection rule, which is two
extra chances for the shipped game to disagree with the board it was dealt — and
that disagreement would look to a player like a wrong answer, not like a bug.

They now import one module. The refactor's proof was that regenerating the year
produced a byte-identical file. The gate gained something real from it too: it used
to verify its own private copy of the rule, so a pass was a statement about the
gate. It now verifies the code that ships, and replays all 365 committed boards
through it — which closes a hole where a stale or hand-edited `boards.json` would
have sailed through the build untouched.
