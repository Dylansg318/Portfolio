A ball rolls onto a pool table at forty-five degrees, bounces three times off
anything solid — the rails or the block in the middle — and drops into the first
pocket it reaches. Fourteen pockets, four guesses. Each miss rolls the ball forward
one bounce, so a wrong guess still buys you something.

The name is the pool rule. Under call-shot rules you name the pocket before you
shoot, and the cushions on the way.

You can also chalk the route yourself. Drag across the cloth and the line goes
where your hand went. When the real path shows up you get both lines and a number
for how far off you were, which is a much harder question than which pocket. One
board a day, then it's spent until tomorrow — or the table can build another one
on the spot.

## Where a new puzzle comes from

The hard part isn't the puzzle. It's having a fresh one every morning that's the
same kind of hard as yesterday's, or the score you post doesn't mean anything.

Every board has exactly three bounces. That keeps Tuesday comparable to Monday,
and it's also what nearly killed the idea: on a plain rectangular table there are
only about 91 different puzzles. Three months and the game's over. One solid block
in the middle of the table took that to 1,037, a bit under three years, and it
doesn't ask anything new of the player. Bouncing off solid things is already the
game.

The puzzle-maker just tries everything. Every combination of table size, block
shape, block position and where the ball comes in — **170,356 boards** — gets rolled,
and anything not worth playing gets thrown out: the ball hits an exact corner and
you can't tell what happened (100,472), a leg too short to notice or too long to
count (39,964), the ball never touches the block (12,092), and so on. 6,392 make it
through. Then it turned out **84% of those were mirror images of each other**, so one
of each pair goes. **1,037 left, about 0.6% of what went in.**

Those get sorted by difficulty into seven equal groups, one per weekday. Easiest
Monday, hardest Saturday, Sunday a step back down, the way crosswords do it.

Drawing is the other thing to do with a board. Guessing the pocket is a
one-in-fourteen shot; drawing the route isn't. Drag across the cloth, and when the
real path appears you see both lines and how far off you were. An eraser rubs it
out in three scrubs if you want another go.

When the day's board is spent, a button builds another one right there. It has to
pass the same tests as the ones from the year, so it's a real puzzle, it just
doesn't count for the day. The table clears itself like a machine: the pockets
close, the block sinks through the cloth, a new section opens up, and the new
pockets get fired out from the middle and bounce off the rails until they land.

There's a **Hard** key on the scorecard too. Same table, but the ball comes in at
any angle, bounces anywhere from two to six times, and the card never says how
many. Each miss still shows you one more bounce — and if there were none left to
show, the ball drops in and that's the day. The daily's "three" was doing more
work than it looked: it told you when to stop tracing, and it caught you when a
slipped line reached a pocket too early. Without it, both are on you. Small sight
marks appear on the rails for it, the way a real table has them, because judging
an odd angle by eye needs a scale. The hard board isn't shipped at all: it's made
from the date on your own device in a fraction of a second, so it's the same for
everyone and it never runs out. It keeps its own score and its own share slip.

And every one of the 365 boards that shipped gets re-played by a separate checker
before the site builds. If the ball doesn't land where the puzzle says, the build
stops.

## The rule

The ball moves on the 45° lattice: one unit sideways for every unit forward. So
it's on a whole number of dots on both axes at every step, it can't slip through a
surface, and there's no tolerance anywhere to tune — on the daily board; hard mode
gives that up on purpose, below. A block face doesn't need its
own rule either. An axis-aligned surface flips the perpendicular component and
leaves the other alone, whichever side you're on, which is why the block adds
nothing for the player to learn.

## Why the tables are odd sizes

I assumed a bigger table meant a harder puzzle. It doesn't. What matters is
gcd(W, H), because it decides how much of the rim the ball can reach before its
cycle closes and it starts retracing itself:

| gcd(W, H) | rim the ball can reach |
|---|---|
| 1 | ~50% |
| 2 | ~41% |
| 3 | ~34–38% |
| 4 | ~28% |
| 6 | ~19% |

A 12×6 has more cloth than an 11×9 and is worth less: fewer reachable pockets,
fewer boards, and a visible bias in where the ball ends up. Every shipped table —
10×7, 11×7, 11×8, 11×9, 10×9, 12×7, 13×9, 13×10 — is coprime, and the generator
throws if you add one that isn't.

## Where the boards come from

Brute force with hard filters. Every combination of table size, block shape, block
position, entry point and direction gets walked, and most of it goes:

| | boards |
|---|---|
| considered | 170,356 |
| a bounce landed exactly on a corner | −100,472 |
| a leg shorter than 2 dots or longer than 7 | −39,964 |
| the ball never touches the block | −12,092 |
| the fourth contact was the block, not a pocket | −5,352 |
| the answer sits too close to the entry | −4,140 |
| the ball crosses its own answer early | −1,488 |
| fourteen spread pockets won't fit | −456 |
| **survived** | **6,392** |
| mirror images of a board already kept | −5,355 |
| **shipped** | **1,037** |

Corners alone are 59%. A ball arriving dead on a corner reflects off both faces at
once, which is correct and unreadable on screen. Two of the filters are about
honesty, not solvability: if the ball rolls over its own answer on an earlier leg
it would've dropped in there, so the stated answer is a lie, and a block the ball
never touches is furniture that looks like it matters.

The mirrors surprised me. 84% of everything that passed was a flip of a board
already in the list, same puzzle with different letters. Each board reduces to one
identity under the four reflections of a rectangle and one per family ships. That's
what turned "about five years of supply" into 2.8, and it's the number I'd have got
wrong if I hadn't checked.

## The clever version I didn't ship

On a bare rectangle you never simulate. Unfold the table — mirror it instead of
bouncing the ball — and the path is a straight line, so rail contacts fall where
`t` is congruent to minus the entry coordinate, mod the width or the height. Two
arithmetic progressions merged, no loop.

The block breaks that. Its faces aren't on either progression, and a bounce off one
restarts the ball from a new point, so everything downstream would have to be
re-derived. The shipped version steps one diagonal unit at a time and checks what
it's touching. The whole search is about eight million steps and runs in a fifth
of a second, so the closed form would've bought nothing measurable and added a
second way to be wrong.

## Grading the year

Difficulty is weighted by what actually costs the player: total dots to count, the
single longest leg (one long count is where you slip), how many bounces are off
the block, how uneven the legs are, and how crowded the answer's neighbourhood is.
Scores split into seven equal-count tiers, not fixed thresholds, so Saturday is
the hardest seventh of the boards that exist rather than the ones over a cutoff I
guessed at. A deterministic shuffle inside each tier means the same commit deals
the same year. All 365 days drew from the tier their weekday asked for.

The answer isn't in the file. The browser has to trace the path anyway to animate
it, so a stored pocket index would be redundant data that also happens to be
tomorrow's answer. The generator asserts it instead. One limit I'll be straight
about: a prerendered site ships the whole year's boards to anyone who opens the
demo, so a determined reader can work out next Tuesday. That's the architecture,
and a puzzle for fun doesn't need me to pretend otherwise.

## Drawing your own line

The cloth takes a freehand line — points are wherever the pointer went, no
snapping, no smoothing — because the whole point is a hand-drawn curve next to an
exact one.

Scoring it is where the judgement is. Average distance from each drawn point to
the nearest point on the true path looks fine and is worthless: a one-inch scribble
sitting on the path scores nearly perfect. So it runs both ways, drawing against
path and path against drawing, averaged. The second half is what makes a line that
didn't go anywhere score like one.

Drawing is a toggle, and that's a phone decision. A finger can only draw where the
page gives up scrolling (`touch-action: none`), and the cloth is most of a phone
screen, so always-on would trap anyone who scrolled onto it. It defaults on for a
mouse and off for touch. Two bugs here only existed on the input I wasn't using:

- Capturing the pointer on `pointerdown` retargets the `click` that follows, so
  every pocket quietly stopped taking a tap while chalk was armed. Capture now
  happens on the first move.
- `preventDefault()` on `pointerdown` suppresses the synthesised click entirely, so
  on a phone a tap on a pocket did nothing. That moved to the move handler too.

The eraser doesn't travel. It lands on the middle of the longest stroke and
shakes, and every stroke on the cloth breaks up anyway, in three scrubs with a
beat between them. The first version swept across the table and had a real bug: a
piece the sweep flew past in its last few percent never got damaged, so the erase
ended with a bright tail still standing. Once the eraser stayed put, damage
stopped depending on position and that whole class of bug went away.

## The table that racks itself

Past the daily board there's a button that generates another. It has to be the
same game, so the definition of a fair board — sizes, the eight filters, pocket
layout, difficulty score — moved out of the build script into a module the demo
imports. Generator and browser call the same `evaluate`, and regenerating the year
afterwards produced a byte-identical file, which is how I know the move changed
nothing.

In the browser it's rejection sampling: pick a random block, entry and direction,
judge it, try again on a fail. About 3.7% survive, so it lands in roughly 27
attempts, 0.039 ms per board. The six tier cutoffs ship in the JSON so a practice
board can name its tier on the same scale.

The pockets at the end get fired out of the centre and bounce off the rails to
their exact spot in the rim. They're not simulated: take the straight line to a
mirrored image of the target and fold it back into the table with a triangle wave,
and you get a real bouncing path that ends exactly where the pocket goes. Same
unfolding trick as above, used as an animation.

Two bugs in the transition, both invisible in the code and obvious on screen. An
SVG gradient defaults to `objectBoundingBox`, so a small cloth panel got the whole
sweep of light squeezed into it and read as a patch; `userSpaceOnUse` fixed it.
And the old chalk, bounce marks and crossed-out pockets were faded to zero, the
board swapped, and then faded back in with the previous puzzle still on them.
Opacity isn't a reset.

## Hard mode, off the grid

The grid was never needed for the bounce. A constant-velocity tracer — real-valued
position, which surface comes first, flip the perpendicular component — reproduces
every one of the 365 dealt boards to the bit, and the gate now proves the same on
all 25,448 lattice boards it sweeps. What the grid bought was everything else: a
finite space to count and dedupe, pockets that either are the contact point or
aren't, and a puzzle you could count when the dots were on the cloth. Those are
the right constraints for a daily with a provable supply. They're the wrong ones
for a mode whose point is variety.

So hard mode lets all three go. Any entry angle from 12° off the rail. Two to six
bounces, climbing through the week in ranges rather than fixed numbers, and never
printed — the daily's "three" is a checksum on your trace, and every board has a
pocket one step from an earlier bounce for a slipped trace to stop in. A miss
with no bounce left to show rolls the ball into the pocket and ends the day, so
you don't know whether the next miss is the last one. And no `boards.json`: the
board is made from the date with a seeded PRNG on the player's device, by
rejection, and it's the same board on every device because the seed is the date.

Exactness becomes margins, and the margins are physical things. The answer lands
within 0.3 dots of its pocket's centre; every earlier rail contact stays a full dot
from every other mouth; nothing comes within 0.6 of a corner, because on screen
that reads as a corner hit whether or not it is one. Fourteen mouths on a 30-point
rim leave almost no room for a long path, which is why 10×7 tops out at four
bounces and the count steps down there — three days a year on the dealt calendar.
A two-bounce board lands in about a hundred attempts, a six-bounce one in a few
thousand on a big table and thirty thousand on 11×7, under a tenth of a second.
One thing I got wrong first: drawing the count per attempt let short paths, which
clear the margins far more easily, crowd out the long ones — forty of sixty boards
came out two-bounce. The count is drawn once, then the search is for a board with
that count.

Sight diamonds go on the rails for it, one per dot, the way a real table carries
them for bank shots. The daily took its dots off the cloth so you'd see the angle
rather than count it, and at 45° the eye can. At a free angle it can't, and a
scale on the wood gives one back without putting anything on the cloth.

## The gate, and the test that was wrong

The physics gate checks seven invariants over every board shape the generator can
produce — 25,448 boards, 1.55 million ball positions, 0.19 seconds. Six are the
obvious ones: the ball stays on the table, never ends up inside the block, never
tunnels through a face, every step is one diagonal unit, direction flips at a
surface and only there. The seventh is reversibility: run the path backwards from
its end and it has to retrace itself exactly.

That one failed on 32% of boards, and the test was the bug. Reversing at a contact
means negating the ray the ball *arrived* on, not the one it left on, and I'd
negated the wrong one. Changing a test until it passes is usually how you ship a
bug with a green check, so it mattered that I could say exactly which line was
wrong.

The generator, the gate and the demo each used to have their own copy of the
reflection rule, which is two extra chances for the shipped game to disagree with
the board it was dealt — and to a player that's a wrong answer, not a bug. They
import one module now. The gate verifies the code that ships, replays all 365
committed boards through it, and generates 2,000 more through the runtime path
with every rule re-derived from scratch, so a practice board that skipped a filter
gets caught. For hard mode it does two more things: runs the off-grid tracer on
every lattice board and demands bit-for-bit agreement with the stepping one, and
makes hard boards from fixed seeds — 373 of them, two to six bounces on every
table — re-deriving each margin from the traced path and running every free path
backwards to a billionth of a dot.
